Slow computation may mean things are being moved slowly

Why does a GPU with more compute not necessarily make a task proportionally faster?

Thinking of a GPU as a factory is only good for holding on to one limited analogy: computation is like machining, reads and writes are like transport. Doubling the machines while transport is still blocked does not make the whole thing proportionally faster. Real analysis has to go back to the number of operations, the bytes moved, the device's rates and the measurements, not to some advertised peak.

Compute The total operations required.
Bandwidth Bytes transferable per unit time.
Throughput Work completed per unit time, not the time needed by one request.
Communication The cost of exchanging data between devices.

Input and conditions of use: Fixed teaching task and ideal rates. Frames two and three independently restore the baseline and change one factor; no actual runtime is claimed.

Start by separating the three accounts

The amount of computation is how many operations have to be done; the amount of storage is how much data has to be kept; the amount of data movement is how much data has to be moved between storage levels or devices. Storing less does not necessarily mean computing less, and adding parallelism does not necessarily make one request faster.

Start by telling three ledgers apart: the items distinguished
Object oneAmount of computation: how many operations
Object twoAmount of storage: how many bytes it occupies
Object threeAmount of movement: how many bytes are read and written

Key difference: Computing fast, fitting in memory and moving data are three ledgers, and the bottleneck may be an entirely different one in each.

Easy to misread: Cutting the parameter count does not necessarily cut end-to-end time in proportion.

Start with the objects and symbols used here
FLOPs and FLOP/s: a count and a rate
FLOPs usually means a number of floating-point operations; FLOP/s means a rate of operations per second. Papers and vendors may count a multiply-add as one operation or two, so the basis has to be fixed. Dividing total operations by the theoretical peak is no promise of real elapsed time: movement, queueing, communication and actual utilization cost something too.

FLOP usually means one floating-point operation; FLOPS is the number of floating-point operations per second. One S apart, yet one is an amount of work and the other a rate. Whether one multiply-add counts as two FLOP depends on the counting convention.

What O(T²) actually means

Big-O complexity describes the order at which the amount of work grows as the input gets larger. If T queries each have to be compared with T keys, there are T² pairings. Double T and the number of pairings becomes four times as large, which is why the attention-score part is commonly said to grow quadratically in T.

Key difference: What is meant here is the growth in the number of attention-score pairings with length, not that every computation becomes exactly four times as much.

Easy to misread: Big-O describes the order of growth, not the seconds a run actually takes.

This does not say that some implementation must take T² seconds, nor does it guarantee that the end-to-end time becomes exactly four times as long, because the hardware, the hidden width, memory movement and the other layers all play a part. When a complexity is written down, say which variable is growing and what is held fixed for the moment.

Why enough storage may still not mean fast computation

Hardware has an upper limit on compute capability, and also an upper limit on the bandwidth at which it reads data per second. If a lot of data is read for only a few operations, bandwidth may be the limit; if the data can be reused while a great deal is computed, compute capability may be the limit.

Why enough storage may still not mean fast computation: the items distinguished
Approach / object AThe computation lower bound: C/R
Approach / object BThe bandwidth lower bound: M/BW
Approach / object CThe total time is bounded below by the larger of the two

Key difference: Before optimizing, ask whether the real bottleneck is compute capability or memory bandwidth.

Easy to misread: In practice there is also the overhead of queueing, scheduling and synchronization.

Estimate with a rough lower bound: a task does C operations and moves M bytes, at a computation rate R and a bandwidth BW, so the time is at least constrained by max(C/R, M/BW). This line of thinking belongs to roofline analysis. In practice there is also queueing and synchronization overhead, so it cannot be taken as an exact timing formula.

What FlashAttention actually saves

Start with the objects and symbols used here
FlashAttention: changing how intermediate data is stored and read
By means such as tiling and online normalization, it avoids keeping the full attention score/weight matrix in slower VRAM for long; the target is still the same attention computation, and floating-point rounding can vary with the order of operations. It does not compress tokens permanently, and it does not skip any Key. Keep "not writing the whole table to VRAM" apart from "not doing this arithmetic at all".

A plain conceptual implementation forms a large T×T table of scores or weights explicitly. By means such as tiling, FlashAttention manages local blocks and the normalization statistics as it computes, so that the whole intermediate table is not written to and read from external VRAM over and over.

Key difference: FlashAttention rearranges the computation and the data access so that the whole attention matrix is not stored and read repeatedly.

Easy to misread: It is not an approximate attention that "reads only the first few tokens".

It does not simply turn exact attention into reading a few tokens, and it is not the same as a KV cache. The first arranges the reads and writes of one attention computation; the second reuses the past K/V across generation steps. Advanced Q027 derives the online softmax; for now, understand it as "the same target, with a different intermediate storage and access order".

PagedAttention and continuous batching

Paging cuts the cache into fixed-size blocks and uses a mapping table to remember which physical block each sequence's logical block corresponds to, which helps manage requests of different lengths and shared prefixes. The management idea resembles that of operating-system paging, but the object here is KV data, not all the memory of a whole program.

Key difference: PagedAttention keeps the logical positions contiguous while letting the physical blocks be scattered, which cuts waste in allocation.

Easy to misread: It is not the same as putting the whole neural network into operating-system paging.

Continuous batching lets a request release its slot once it finishes so that a new request can join, rather than requiring the whole batch to end together. It can raise resource utilization, but prefill, decode, and long and short requests still have to be scheduled against one another.

What exactly is split across multiple GPUs

Start with the objects and symbols used here
DP, TP, PP, ZeRO and FSDP
Data parallelism lets several copies of the model process different samples and synchronize their gradients; tensor parallelism splits the parameters and the computation of a single operator; pipeline parallelism puts different layers on different devices. ZeRO/FSDP-style techniques also shard parameters, gradients or optimizer state. What they split differs: dividing one layer's matrix in two is not the same as giving two independent user tasks to two agents.
Method How it mainly divides The cost you have to keep track of
Data parallelism DP Several copies of the model handle different samples Synchronizing gradients and the like during training
Tensor parallelism TP Split a matrix or an operator across several devices Communication and combination within a layer
Pipeline parallelism PP Different layers on different devices Waiting between stages, and transfer
ZeRO-style sharding Spread the training parameters, gradients or optimizer state The overhead of fetching and synchronizing

More devices is not an unconditional speed-up. If one round of communication takes longer than the computation it saves, the result can be slower. Nor can sharding in training and separating services in inference be covered by the same word.

Training saves, inference caches and checkpoints

Start with the objects and symbols used here
checkpoint: you must look at the object that was saved
A model checkpoint saves weights or training state; activation checkpointing saves a few intermediate nodes so they can be recomputed on the backward pass; an agent task checkpoint saves how far execution has got. The three share a name, but what they restore is entirely different. When you see "checkpoint", first ask whether it restores parameters, recomputes activations, or continues a task. An agent saving a summary does not update model weights.

Training may keep activations in order to compute gradients; activation checkpointing saves some nodes and recomputes the rest on the backward pass, trading computation for memory. A KV cache, by contrast, trades memory for repeated inference computation. The two even embody opposite resource trade-offs.

A model checkpoint on disk saves the weights or training state needed to restore it; an agent task checkpoint saves how far the work has got. Both are called a checkpoint, yet what they save is entirely different.

Worked example · Lesson 62

Count the computation and the movement first, then decide whether the GPU is short of compute

Input and conditions. A hypothetical operation does 1,000,000 floating-point operations and moves 8,000,000 bytes. The hypothetical device peaks at 100GFLOP/s with a bandwidth of 10GB/s, in decimal units.

Steps and objectsInternal actionIntermediate result / new state
1 Compute the pure computation lower bound10^6/10^11 seconds0.00001 seconds, that is 10 microseconds.
2 Compute the pure movement lower bound8×10^6/10^10 seconds0.0008 seconds, that is 800 microseconds.
3 Take the larger bottleneckWith ideal full overlap, take the maxAt least about 800 microseconds, not 10 microseconds.
4 Reduce the movementIf a fused operation needs only 4MBThe movement lower bound is about 400 microseconds; it can run faster even with the compute unchanged.
5 Switch to quadratic pairingT goes from 100 to 200The attention position pairs go from 10,000 to 40,000, not merely double.

Join the steps up. "Saving memory reads and writes" and "cutting the number of mathematical pairings" are different optimizations. A high GPU peak figure is no reason to overlook the movement.

Limits and counterexamples. These are idealized lower bounds, not actual performance; scheduling, communication, kernel launches, cache hits and imperfect overlap are not yet counted.

End-of-lesson self-check

Self-check 1

If the input T doubles, how does the number of T×T relationships change? Does that mean the whole system must be four times slower?

Show the answerHide the answer
The number of relationships becomes four times as large; the end-to-end time also depends on the other computation, the hardware and the scheduling, so no factor is guaranteed directly.
Self-check 2

Can FlashAttention, KV cache and quantization substitute for one another?

Show the answerHide the answer
No. They change, respectively, the intermediate computation and the reads and writes inside attention, the reuse of past computation, and the precision of the numeric representation; they act on different objects.
